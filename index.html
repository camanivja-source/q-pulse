2025 Nobel Prize in Physics

The Nobel science behind Q-Pulse

In 1984 and 1985, at the University of California, Berkeley, John Clarke, Michel H. Devoret and John M. Martinis ran a series of experiments on a superconducting circuit. They showed that a circuit big enough to hold in your hand can follow the strange rules of quantum physics. Their two discoveries are the two halves of our sensor.

Discovery 1 · Macroscopic quantum tunnellingDiscovery 2 · Energy quantisation
SuperconductorThin insulating barrierPaired electrons, moving in step

The heart of the experiment is a Josephson junction. When the metal is cooled until it superconducts, its electrons pair up and flow with no resistance, right across the barrier.

Discovery 1

A whole circuit walks through a wall

Roll a ball at a hill without enough energy and it always rolls back. Quantum particles can cheat: sometimes they turn up on the far side, as if they had tunnelled straight through. Before these experiments, that had only been seen for single particles.

Inside a superconducting Josephson junction, billions of paired electrons move together and behave like one giant particle: an artificial atom. The laureates showed that this whole circuit tunnels through its energy barrier instead of climbing over it. The proof is electrical. The circuit jumps from zero voltage to a voltage you can measure.

Discovery 2

Energy comes in exact packets

The same circuit cannot take in just any amount of energy. Like an atom, it has fixed energy levels, and it only absorbs a microwave whose frequency matches the gap between two of them. Think of a lock that opens for one exact key.

The Q-Pulse chip is tuned so that key is 5.0000 GHz. Its lock window is razor thin, 4.99993 to 5.00040 GHz, about one part in ten thousand. Anything outside it bounces off, however strong it is.

The frequency bridge

Turning a whisper into the right key

Here is the problem. A growing micro-crack clicks at ultrasonic frequencies of 30 kHz to 500 kHz. The quantum chip only listens at 5 GHz, about 50,000 times higher.

The fix is a trick every radio uses: mixing. Q-Pulse combines the crack's vibration with a steady 4.9999 GHz microwave carrier, and the output is the sum of the two. A 100 kHz crack tone is 0.0001 GHz, so 4.9999 + 0.0001 = 5.0000 GHz, exactly the chip's sweet spot.

Wind and traffic shake a building at only 1 Hz to 100 Hz. Added to the carrier they barely move it, so they miss the lock completely.

The twist

A weakness becomes the sensor

Quantum computers: block the vibration

A quantum computer's chips are so delicate that outside vibration knocks them out of their quantum state and causes errors. Engineers go to great lengths to shield and isolate them.

Q-Pulse: listen to the vibration

We want that sensitivity. Q-Pulse feeds one chosen kind of vibration in on purpose, through the frequency bridge, so the only thing that can disturb the chip is the sound of a crack. The same effect that ruins a calculation reveals hidden danger.

Head to head with today's sensors

Engineers already listen for cracks with piezoelectric acoustic sensors. They work, and they are cheap and rugged. Q-Pulse aims at the signals they cannot pick out of the noise.

Judge FAQ: the tough questions

How does John Clarke's formula let engineers tune the chip?

The formula says the junction's natural frequency depends on three things we control. The critical current I₀ and the capacitance C are fixed when the chip is made, and they set its top frequency. The bias current I can be changed while the chip is running: more bias tilts the energy landscape and slides the frequency down.

For example, I₀ = 5 µA and C = 5 pF give 8.77 GHz with no bias. Raising the bias to 94.6% of I₀ brings the chip to exactly 5.0000 GHz. Try it in the lab.

How can a kHz crack trigger a GHz quantum chip?

By adding frequencies. The up-converter mixes the crack's ultrasonic tone with a steady 4.9999 GHz carrier, and its output contains the sum of the two. A 100 kHz tone is 0.0001 GHz, so the sum is 5.0000 GHz, inside the chip's lock window.

Radios have shifted signals up and down in frequency this way for about a century. The crack only has to nudge the carrier the last tiny step.

Why doesn't heavy traffic set off false alarms?

Traffic and wind shake structures at roughly 1 to 100 Hz. Mixed with the carrier, a 60 Hz truck rumble lands at 4.99990006 GHz, almost 30 kHz short of the lock window.

Energy quantisation means the circuit can only absorb packets of exactly the right size, so a wrong frequency is reflected however strong it is. In the simulator you can turn a truck up to full strength and the chip still sits at zero volts.

Why does the chip need cooling?

Two reasons. First, the metal only superconducts when it is very cold: below about 1.2 K for aluminium and 9.2 K for niobium.

Second, heat is random energy. One energy packet at 5 GHz equals the heat energy of just 0.24 K, so the chip has to sit far below that, around 0.02 K. Any warmer and heat would kick the circuit over the wall at random, which would look like false alarms. Switch the cooling off in the simulator to watch the sensor go blind.

Where does the frozen chip sit on a building or a bridge?

Not inside the concrete. Small ultrasonic pickups are fixed to the structure at room temperature. Cables carry their signals to one cabinet that holds the up-converter and the cryostat with the chip. One cold chip can listen to many pickups in turn.

Is Q-Pulse a finished product?

No. Q-Pulse is a concept design. The quantum effects it relies on are proven, and they earned the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics.

The next steps would be a bench prototype that couples an ultrasonic pickup and a mixer to a real junction, a measurement of how sharp the lock really is, and a compact cooler that can live in an equipment cabinet.
